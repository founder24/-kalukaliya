A physical quantity is any objective property of a material or physical system that can be quantified through experimental measurement. Evolution of Systems of Units: CGS, FPS, MKS, and SI
Historically, different scientific and geographical communities developed localized systems of base units:
 * CGS System: Centimetre (\text{cm}), Gram (\text{g}), Second (\text{s}).
 * FPS System: Foot (\text{ft}), Pound (\text{lb}), Second (\text{s}).
 * MKS System: Metre (\text{m}), Kilogram (\text{kg}), Second (\text{s}).
The modern standard is the Système International d'Unités (SI), maintained under the authority of the Bureau International des Poids et Mesures (BIPM) and adopted globally. The SI is a coherent decimal system, meaning derived units are produced without non-unity proportional factors, and numerical conversions within the system require only powers of 10.

Supplementary Geometric Quantities: Plane Angle and Solid Angle
To describe angular geometry without introducing independent base mechanical dimensions, SI defines two supplementary geometric quantities:

                 ds (arc)                                dA (area)

             /                                         /   _---\
     r     /                                   r     / _-       \

 * Plane Angle (d\theta): The ratio of circular arc length subtended (ds) to the radius of curvature (r):
   
   
   For a full circle: \theta = \frac{2\pi r}{r} = 2\pi\text{ rad} = 360^\circ (1\text{ rad} \approx 57.296^\circ).
 * Solid Angle (d\Omega): The ratio of the intercepted surface area (dA) of a spherical shell to the square of its radius (r^2):
   
   
   For a complete closed spherical surface: \Omega = \frac{4\pi r^2}{r^2} = 4\pi\text{ sr}.
 * Dimensional Status: Because [d\theta] = \frac{[L]}{[L]} = [M^0 L^0 T^0] and [d\Omega] = \frac{[L^2]}{[L^2]} = [M^0 L^0 T^0], both quantities are dimensionless, yet possess derived units within SI.

 * Non-Zero Digits: Every non-zero digit is significant.
 * Interior Trapped Zeros: Zeros situated between two non-zero digits are always significant, regardless of decimal point placement (e.g., 6.032 has 4 SF).
 * Leading Zeros: For decimal numbers strictly less than 1, zeros to the right of the decimal point and to the left of the first non-zero digit are non-significant placeholders indicating decimal scale (e.g., in 0.0006032, the four underlined zeros are not significant; it has 4 SF).
 * Trailing Zeros:
   * In numbers with an explicit decimal point, trailing zeros are significant (e.g., 6.320\text{ J} has 4 SF; 0.2370\text{ g cm}^{-3} has 4 SF).
   * In whole numbers without a decimal point, trailing zeros are non-significant (e.g., 123\,000\text{ m} has 3 SF).
 * Scientific Notation (a \times 10^b): Ambiguity in trailing zeros is avoided by writing measurements in normalized scientific notation (1 \le a < 10). All digits appearing in the mantissa a are significant; the power of ten 10^b sets only the order of magnitude:

The Even-Odd Rounding Algorithm for Dropped Digit 5
 * If the dropped digit is > 5, increment the preceding digit by 1.
 * If the dropped digit is < 5, leave the preceding digit unchanged.
 * If the dropped digit is exactly 5 (or 5 followed exclusively by zeros):
   * Preceding digit is ODD: Increment by 1 (e.g., 2.735 \to \mathbf{2.74}).
   * Preceding digit is EVEN: Leave unchanged (e.g., 2.745 \to \mathbf{2.74}).

The Principle of Homogeneity of Dimensions
The Principle of Homogeneity states that a physical equation is dimensionally valid if and only if each additive term on both sides has identical dimensions:


Physical quantities with different dimensions cannot be added or subtracted; velocity cannot be added to force, nor can mass be subtracted from temperature.
                   TESTING EQUATION VALIDITY (HOMOGENEITY)
                                      |
                      x = x₀ + v₀·t + (1/2)·a·t²[span_163](start_span)[span_163](end_span)
                                      |
         +----------------------------+----------------------------+
         |                            |                            |
       [x₀]                        [v₀·t]                      [(1/2)·a·t²]
        ||                           ||                             ||
       [L]                      [LT⁻¹] · [T]                   [LT⁻²] · [T²]
        ||                           ||                             ||
       [L]                          [L]                            [L][span_164](start_span)[span_164](end_span)

Because every additive term simplifies to length [L], the equation is dimensionally consistent.

Conceptual Diagnostic: Systematic Limits and Failure Modes of Dimensional Analysis [Summary Point 11 & High-Yield Trap]
Dimensional analysis is a powerful consistency test, but it has distinct limitations:
 * Dimensionless Multipliers: It cannot determine dimensionless constants of proportionality (e.g., factors like 2\pi or \frac{1}{2} must be determined through experiment or calculus).
 * Transcendental Arguments: It cannot evaluate arguments of transcendental functions (\sin \theta, \cos(\omega t), e^{-kt}, \ln(kx)), which must be dimensionless.
 * Variable Number Limitations: In mechanics, which relies on three base dimensions (M, L, T), it cannot solve for relations involving more than three mutually independent variables.
 * Additive Formulations: It cannot derive equations containing sums of distinct terms (e.g., s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2).
 * Physical Ambiguity: It cannot distinguish between distinct physical quantities that share the same dimensions (such as work and torque, or frequency and angular velocity).
