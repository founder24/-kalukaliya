The Point-Particle Approximation
In classical mechanics, real, extended bodies have finite geometry and can undergo simultaneous translation, rotation, and internal vibration. However, an extended object is mathematically approximated as a point particle whenever its spatial dimensions are negligible compared to the total distance traversed during the time interval under consideration.
 * Valid Approximations: A multi-carriage railway train traveling hundreds of kilometers between distant stations; a monkey perched on the shoulder of a cyclist riding smoothly along a large circular velodrome; the Earth orbiting the Sun (R_{\text{Earth}} \ll \text{Orbital Radius}).
 * Invalid Approximations: A spinning cricket ball that swerves in air and turns abruptly upon pitching (rotational spin and surface impact dynamics govern its behavior); a tumbling glass beaker slipping off a laboratory desk (rotational orientation and container size are comparable to the fall height).
Coordinate Systems and Directional Conventions
Position in one dimension is specified by choosing an arbitrary origin O along a reference line:
 * Positions to the right of O are algebraically assigned a positive sign (+x).
 * Positions to the left of O are algebraically assigned a negative sign (-x).
 * The choice of origin and positive direction is arbitrary, but once established, it must be maintained consistently throughout all subsequent dynamical equations.

Mathematical Definitions
Average Velocity (\bar{v})
Average velocity is the net displacement \Delta x divided by the finite time interval \Delta t:


On a position-time (x-t) plot, \bar{v} equals the geometric slope of the secant line passing through coordinates (t_1, x_1) and (t_2, x_2). Its algebraic sign reflects the direction of motion along the coordinate axis.
Average Speed (v_{\text{avg}})
Average speed is the total path length traversed divided by the total time elapsed:


Because s \ge |\Delta x|, average speed is always greater than or equal to the magnitude of average velocity (v_{\text{avg}} \ge |\bar{v}|).
Instantaneous Velocity (v) & Instantaneous Speed
Instantaneous velocity is defined as the limiting value of average velocity as the time interval \Delta t becomes infinitesimally small:


In calculus, \frac{dx}{dt} is the first derivative of position with respect to time.

Conceptual Diagnostic: Truth-Value Analysis of Kinematic Statements [Textbook Exercise 2.7 & Points to Ponder]
 * Statement (a): "A particle with zero speed at an instant may have non-zero acceleration."
   * TRUE. When a ball is thrown vertically upward, its speed at the highest point is momentarily zero (v = 0), yet its acceleration remains non-zero and equals the acceleration due to gravity (a = -g = -9.8\text{ m s}^{-2}).
 * Statement (b): "A particle with zero speed may have non-zero velocity."
   * FALSE. Speed is the absolute magnitude of velocity (v_{\text{speed}} = \vert{}\vec{v}\vert{}). If speed is zero, the magnitude of velocity is identically zero, so velocity cannot be non-zero.
 * Statement (c): "A particle with constant speed must have zero acceleration."
   * FALSE in general; TRUE for 1D. In one dimension, constant speed implies constant velocity along the line, so a = 0. In two or three dimensions, an object in uniform circular motion moves at constant speed while undergoing centripetal acceleration.
 * Statement (d): "A particle with positive acceleration must be speeding up."
   * FALSE. Whether an object speeds up or slows down depends on the relative signs of velocity and acceleration. If velocity is negative (v < 0) and acceleration is positive (a > 0), the acceleration opposes the motion, causing the object to slow down.
