Source: Gauhati University Degree Examination

“The great defect of our modern civilization is that it does not know what to do with its wonderful knowledge. Science has given us powers fit for the gods, yet we use them like small children. For example, we do not know how to manage our machines. Machines were made to be man’s servants; yet he has grown so dependent upon them that they are in a fair way to become his masters. They must be fed with coal, and given petrol to drink, and oil to wash with, and they must be kept at the right temperature. Title: Man’s Subservience to Machines

Modern civilization has failed to use its scientific power wisely. Although machines were invented to serve humanity, humans have become entirely dependent on them. People now spend much of their lives feeding, maintaining, and tending to machines. As a result, machines have become demanding masters, and any failure to maintain them leads to widespread industrial destruction.

Chapter 1: Charles Lamb — “Dream Children: A Reverie”
1. Authorial Background & Essayistic Milieu
Author Profile: Charles Lamb (1775–1834), one of the foremost essayists of the English Romantic period, published under the pseudonym Elia.
Publication History: First published in The London Magazine in 1822, and subsequently collected in Essays of Elia (1823).
Biographical Tragedy: Lamb’s personal life was marked by severe trauma. In 1796, his beloved sister Mary Lamb (represented as Bridget in his essays), in a fit of temporary insanity, killed their mother with a kitchen knife. To save Mary from lifelong confinement in a public asylum, Charles gave a solemn legal undertaking to act as her guardian. This lifelong sacrifice forced him to abandon his hopes of marriage and family life, remaining a dedicated bachelor.
Unrequited Love: In his youth, Lamb fell deeply in love with Ann Simmons, a young woman from Hertfordshire whom he courted unsuccessfully for seven years. She eventually married a pawnbroker named Bartram. In the essay, she is immortalized as the ethereal mother figure, Alice W---n.
Fraternal Bereavement: The immediate emotional catalyst for the essay was the death of Charles's elder brother, John Lamb (represented as Uncle John L---), in November 1821. John's passing left Charles in profound grief and isolation.
2. Narrative Structure & Form
Genre: Romantic Personal Essay / Confessional Prose.
The "Reverie" Technique: A reverie is a waking daydream or subconscious fantasy. The essay does not announce its fictional nature at the outset; rather, it establishes an affectionate domestic scene between a father and his children before dissolving the illusion in a sudden, haunting awakening.
Stream of Consciousness & Syntax: Written in expansive, continuous sentences joined by fluid conjunctions (“and then,” “here,” “how”). This stylistic choice mirrors the spontaneous, uninterrupted movement of a waking dream.
3. Comprehensive Textual Breakdown

A. The Setting: The Great House in Norfolk
Elia narrates stories of his youth to his two children, Alice and John.
He describes a grand, historic mansion in Norfolk (modeled after Blakesware House in Hertfordshire, where Lamb’s maternal grandmother, Mary Field, was the housekeeper).
The House’s History & Decay: The mansion belonged to a wealthy aristocrat who lived elsewhere. Lamb recalls its grand scale, empty banqueting halls, carved staircases, and marble busts of the Twelve Caesars.
The Tragic Ballad: Carved in wood on the chimney-piece of the great hall was the tragic ballad of The Children in the Wood. Elia notes with sorrow that a foolish, wealthy inheritor later tore down this carved wooden treasure, replacing it with a cold, blank marble chimney-piece containing no story at all.
B. The Portrait of Grandmother Field
Moral Stature & Piety: Grandmother Field was technically only the housekeeper, yet she managed the estate with the dignity and grace of a true mistress. She was universally beloved, deeply religious, and knew the entire Book of Psalms by heart.
Her Youth & Tragic End: In her youth, she was celebrated as the finest dancer in the county until a stoic, painful battle with cancer bowed her frame.
Supernatural Courage: She slept alone in an isolated room of the vast mansion despite local beliefs that the ghosts of two infants haunted the grand staircase. Grandmother Field declared that “our religion would keep us from harm,” and because she was innocent, the spirits never troubled her.
Funeral Reverence: Her funeral was attended by a vast concourse of people, both wealthy gentry and the impoverished villagers from miles around, reflecting her universal respect.
C. The Garden & The Forbidden Fruit
While other boys played or hunted, the solitary, introverted Elia spent entire days wandering through the estate’s vast gardens, watching the dace and carp fish swimming in the orange-tree pond.
The Moral Sanctuary: Elia admired the luscious nectarines, peaches, and grapes basking against the sunny garden walls. However, viewing them with religious awe, he never plucked a single fruit, treating them as forbidden fruit and contenting himself with wandering among the old marble statues.
D. The Elegy for Uncle John L--- (John Lamb)
When the dream children ask to hear about their Uncle John L---, the tone shifts from gentle nostalgia to an elegy for Charles's late brother.
Physical Vitality: Uncle John was handsome, brave, spirited, and Grandmother Field’s favorite. He rode spirited horses, defended younger boys, and carried the young, physically lame Charles for miles upon his back.
Pathos & Remorse: In adulthood, John himself suffered from physical lameness and pain. Charles confesses his private remorse, recalling moments when he grew impatient with John’s moodiness. He mourns his brother’s absence: “I missed his kindness, and I missed his crossness, and wished him to be alive again, to be quarrelling with him (for we quarrelled sometimes), rather than not have him again.”
E. The Unrequited Courtship & The Dissolution
Seeing the children weeping at the tale of their uncle, Elia turns the narrative toward their mother, Alice W---n (Ann Simmons).
He describes how he courted her for seven long, painful years amid hope, doubt, and rejection.
The Climactic Vanishing: As Elia gazes intently at little Alice, he notices her eyes, hair, and expressions looking uncannily identical to the elder Alice. Suddenly, the children begin to grow faint, receding into the background until they appear as distant shadows.
The Haunting Pronouncement: Out of the fading mist, the spectral figures whisper their tragic reality:
“We are not of Alice, nor of thee, nor are we children at all. The children of Alice call Bartram father. We are nothing; less than nothing, and dreams. We are only what might have been, and must wait upon the tedious shores of Lethe millions of ages before we have existence and a name.”

The Awakening: Elia abruptly snaps awake in his bachelor armchair. The warm family hearth dissolves into his quiet, solitary reality. The children are gone, the room is still, and beside him sits his faithful, mentally fragile sister Bridget (Mary Lamb).
4. Key Thematic Pillars
The Interplay of Humor and Pathos: Lamb balances gentle, humorous observations of childhood mannerisms (little Alice copying her mother’s gestures; John splitting a bunch of grapes) against deep emotional tragedy (lameness, cancer, bereavement, and romantic loss).
Nostalgia and the Elasticity of Time: Past, present, and unrealized future blur together. The golden days of Blakesware are resurrected through childhood wonder.
Unfulfilled Domestic Longing: The essay gives voice to the private sorrow of an enforced bachelor whose domestic dreams were sacrificed to care for his family.
Chapter 2: Ruskin Bond — “Coming Home to Dehra”
1. Authorial Background & Regional Milieu
Author Profile: Ruskin Bond (born 1934), an Indian author of British descent, is celebrated for his portraits of the Himalayan foothills, small-town life, and the quiet beauty of everyday existence.
The Genius Loci (Spirit of Place): Dehra (Dehradun), situated in the Doon Valley between the Siwalik hills and the Himalayas, is the emotional home of Bond’s literary imagination.
Biographical Trauma: When Bond was only ten years old, his beloved father, Aubrey Bond (serving with the Royal Air Force), died of malaria and jaundice in Delhi in 1944. Ruskin was called away from his boarding school in Shimla to join his mother, who had remarried a local businessman.
2. Plot Progression & Narrative Architecture

                                           │
        ┌──────────────────────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────┐
        ▼                                  ▼                                  ▼
THE JOURNEY THROUGH THE PAST       THE SENSE OF DISPLACEMENT          THE ECOLOGICAL LOSS
• Arrival at Dehra Station         • Stepfather & remarried mother    • Loss of mango/litchi groves
• Sensory tonga ride               • Emotional detachment at home     • Ancient trees cut for roads

A. The Arrival & Sensory Landscape of Old Dehra
The narrative follows the young boy arriving by train at the Dehradun railway station, passing through the historic Siwalik mountain pass and the Mohand tunnel.
The Tonga Ride: The boy hires a horse-drawn tonga to travel to his family home. Bond captures the sensory textures of old Dehra:
The rhythmic clip-clop of horse hooves on quiet macadam roads.
The jingling of brass harness bells.
The scents in the air: crushed eucalyptus leaves, flowering mango blossoms, wet earth (petrichor), and clean mountain breezes flowing down from Mussoorie.
The clear water running through canals (nullahs) along roads lined with jacaranda, laburnum, and gulmohar trees.
B. The Grandfather’s Bungalow & The Great Banyan Tree
Bond reflects on his early childhood spent in his grandparents’ colonial bungalow.
The Banyan Tree Sanctuary: In his grandfather's compound stood a magnificent, sprawling banyan tree, older than the town itself.
To the lonely, observant boy, this tree was a private kingdom and reading library. Perched in its branches, hidden behind aerial roots and broad leaves, he read books, observed squirrels, and developed his deep bond with nature.
C. Filial Grief & Domestic Displacement
The Loss of the Father: The emotional shadow over the journey is the death of his father. With his father, the boy had experienced deep companionship, shared walks, stamp-collecting, and quiet mutual respect in Delhi.
The Alien Mother's House: Returning to Dehra brings estrangement rather than comfort. His mother had remarried, and the household now centered around his stepfather and their new infant child.
The reunion is polite but emotionally distant. His mother is preoccupied, leaving the boy feeling like an outsider in what had once been his family home. He wanders the garden and lanes to escape the coldness inside.
D. The Urbanization & Environmental Destruction of Dehra
In the essay's reflective second half, Bond contrasts the idyllic Dehra of his youth with the modern, expanding city of recent decades:
The quiet roads where tongas once strolled have been widened into noisy highways crowded with automobiles, trucks, and buses.
The famous litchi and mango orchards that once gave Dehradun its garden character have been cleared for concrete shopping complexes and residential colonies.
Ancient roadside trees that shaded travelers and housed wildlife have been felled in the name of development.
3. Key Thematic Pillars
Childhood Bereavement and Emotional Exile: Explores the isolation of a sensitive boy navigating the sudden loss of his father and finding himself an emotional stranger within his mother's new household.
The Ecological Cost of Progress: A quiet lament for environmental destruction. Modernization brings noise and concrete at the expense of old trees, fresh air, and peaceful living.
Memory as Sanctuary: Even as the physical town changes, the narrator's prose preserves the memory of old Dehra, keeping its past alive.
Chapter 3: Muhammad Yunus — “Towards Creating a Poverty Free World”
1. Authorial Background & Socio-Economic Context
Author Profile: Muhammad Yunus (born 1940), Bangladeshi economist, founder of Grameen Bank, and pioneer of the concepts of microcredit and social business.
Global Recognition: Awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 2006 (jointly with Grameen Bank) for using microfinance to create economic and social development from below.
Historical Genesis: During the devastating 1974 famine in Bangladesh, Yunus, then an economics professor at Chittagong University, realized that sophisticated economic theories failed to explain why impoverished, hard-working villagers remained trapped in destitution. In 1976, in the village of Jobra, he began lending small sums of his own money to local craftspeople, giving birth to the microcredit movement.
2. Core Concepts & Theoretical Paradigms

A. The "Bonsai Tree" Metaphor: The Root Cause of Poverty
Yunus rejects the classical economic assumption that people are poor due to personal incompetence, laziness, or lack of ambition.
The Core Thesis:
“Poverty is not created by poor people. It has been created and sustained by the economic and social systems that we have designed for ourselves.”

The Metaphor: When a seed from a giant forest tree is planted in a tiny flowerpot, it grows into a miniature bonsai tree. The seed itself is not defective; the small pot restricts its root growth and deprives it of nutrients. Similarly, poor people are born with full creative and entrepreneurial potential, but society places them in an institutional "flowerpot"—denying them capital, credit, and legal rights.
B. The Critique of Conventional Banking
Systemic Exclusion: Conventional commercial banks operate on a principle of exclusion, lending only to the wealthy who already have assets.
The Collateral Barrier: Traditional banks require physical collateral, formal employment records, and legal paperwork that the poor do not possess.
Systemic Injustice: Conventional finance drives the poor into the hands of exploitative local moneylenders (loan sharks), who charge exorbitant interest rates that reduce borrowers to bonded labor.
Yunus argues that the financial system is backward: those who need capital most receive the least, while those who need it least receive the most. He insists that conventional banks are not "people-worthy," rather than the poor not being "credit-worthy."
C. The Grameen Bank Model: Mechanics of Microcredit
Collateral-Free Lending: Microcredit provides very small, unsecured loans to destitute individuals for income-generating self-employment (such as basket weaving, livestock rearing, or small trade).
Trust & Mutual Accountability: Replaces legal contracts and lawyers with peer-support groups of five borrowers. If one member struggles, the group steps in to help, achieving repayment rates consistently above 98%—higher than traditional commercial banks.
Focus on Women: Over 95% (nearly 97%) of Grameen Bank borrowers are women.
Empirical Rationale: When loans are given to women, the benefits reach the entire family faster. Women prioritize children's nutrition, healthcare, clothing, and schooling. In contrast, income earned by men is more often spent on immediate personal consumption.
Doorstep Service: Grameen Bank officers travel directly to village homes, ensuring banking comes to the people rather than forcing illiterate villagers to navigate intimidating bureaucratic offices.
D. The Social Business Model
Yunus argues that traditional capitalism has a flawed, one-dimensional view of human nature, assuming people are motivated solely by personal financial profit. He proposes expanding the market system to include Social Business.

Comparative Parameter	Conventional Capitalist Business	Social Business (Type-I)
Primary Mission	Maximizing personal financial profit for owners/shareholders.	Solving a specific social, educational, or environmental problem.
Dividend Policy	Distributes profits as dividends to investors.	Zero dividends; investors recover only their principal investment.
Surplus Utilization	Diverted into private accumulation.	100% reinvested into expanding the business and lowering costs.
Financial Sustainability	Dependent on market competitiveness.	Self-sustaining; covers all costs through market operations (not charity).
E. The Vision: The "Poverty Museum"
Yunus envisions a future where every human being has access to credit, healthcare, and education as basic human rights.
In such a world, poverty will be eradicated entirely. Future generations will only be able to learn about starvation, slums, and destitution by visiting historical "poverty museums."
Chapter 4: Applied Grammar — Voice Change
1. Fundamental Transformation Mechanics
Voice refers to the form of a verb indicating whether the grammatical subject is the doer of the action (Active Voice) or the receiver of the action (Passive Voice).

ACTIVE:   [Subject / Doer]  +  [Transitive Verb]  +  [Object / Receiver]
                 │                                            │
PASSIVE:  [Object as Subject] + [Auxiliary Verb + V3] + [by/to/at] + [Subject as Object]
Core Rules:
The direct object of the active sentence becomes the subject of the passive sentence.
The main verb is always converted into its Past Participle (V_3) form.
The appropriate form of the auxiliary verb ‘be’ (am, is, are, was, were, being, been) is inserted according to the tense and the new subject.
The active subject becomes the passive object, usually introduced by the preposition ‘by’ (or specific prepositions like to, at, with, in).
Sentences with intransitive verbs (verbs without an object, e.g., sleep, arrive, die) cannot be converted into passive voice.
2. Tense-Wise Structural Conversion Matrix
Tense Formulation	Active Voice Architecture	Passive Voice Architecture	Master Conversion Example
Simple Present	S + V_1(s/es) + O	O + \text{am/is/are} + V_3 + \text{by} + S	Active: She writes essays. Passive: Essays are written by her.
Present Continuous	S + \text{am/is/are} + V\text{-ing} + O	O + \text{am/is/are} + \text{being} + V_3 + \text{by} + S	Active: Workers are widening the road. Passive: The road is being widened by workers.
Present Perfect	S + \text{has/have} + V_3 + O	O + \text{has/have} + \text{been} + V_3 + \text{by} + S	Active: They have cleared the meadow. Passive: The meadow has been cleared by them.
Simple Past	S + V_2 + O	O + \text{was/were} + V_3 + \text{by} + S	Active: Lamb wrote the reverie. Passive: The reverie was written by Lamb.
Past Continuous	S + \text{was/were} + V\text{-ing} + O	O + \text{was/were} + \text{being} + V_3 + \text{by} + S	Active: He was driving a tonga. Passive: A tonga was being driven by him.
Past Perfect	S + \text{had} + V_3 + O	O + \text{had} + \text{been} + V_3 + \text{by} + S	Active: She had lost the ring. Passive: The ring had been lost by her.
Simple Future	S + \text{will/shall} + V_1 + O	O + \text{will/shall} + \text{be} + V_3 + \text{by} + S	Active: The bank will disburse loans. Passive: Loans will be disbursed by the bank.
Future Perfect	S + \text{will have} + V_3 + O	O + \text{will have been} + V_3 + \text{by} + S	Active: They will have built the hall. Passive: The hall will have been built by them.
Modal Auxiliaries	S + \text{can/may/must} + V_1 + O	O + \text{modal} + \text{be} + V_3 + \text{by} + S	Active: We must eliminate poverty. Passive: Poverty must be eliminated by us.
3. Special & High-Yield Exception Rules
A. Imperative Sentences (Orders, Requests, Advice)
Direct Commands / Orders: \text{Let} + \text{Object} + \text{be} + V_3
Active: Shut the front door. \to Passive: Let the front door be shut.
Active: Do not pluck the flowers. \to Passive: Let the flowers not be plucked.
Moral Advice / Duty: \text{Object} + \text{should be} + V_3
Active: Help the poor. \to Passive: The poor should be helped.
Active: Respect your elders. \to Passive: Your elders should be respected.
Polite Requests: \text{You are requested to} + V_1 + \text{Object}
Active: Please close the window. \to Passive: You are requested to close the window.
B. Interrogative Sentences
Yes/No Questions: Transform the auxiliary verb to match the new subject:
Active: Did the noise alert the traveler? \to Passive: Was the traveler alerted by the noise?
Active: Has someone cleaned the room? \to Passive: Has the room been cleaned?
'Wh-' Questions: Retain the question word at the beginning:
Active: Why did you reject the proposal? \to Passive: Why was the proposal rejected by you?
The 'Who' Rule: 'Who' changes into ‘By whom’:
Active: Who broke the antique vase? \to Passive: By whom was the antique vase broken?
C. Quasi-Passive Verbs
Verbs that are active in form but passive in meaning.

Structural Formula: \text{Subject} + \text{Auxiliary} + \text{Adjective} + \text{when it is/they are} + V_3
Active: Rose smells sweet. \to Passive: Rose is sweet when it is smelt.
Active: Quinine tastes bitter. \to Passive: Quinine is bitter when it is tasted.
D. Verbs Taking Non-‘By’ Prepositions
Certain verbs take specific prepositions rather than ‘by’ in the passive voice:

Know \to to: Active: I know the author. \to Passive: The author is known to me.
Shock / Surprise \to at: Active: His behavior shocked everyone. \to Passive: Everyone was shocked at his behavior.
Contain / Include \to in: Active: This book contains useful ideas. \to Passive: Useful ideas are contained in this book.
Please / Satisfy \to with: Active: His honesty pleased the teacher. \to Passive: The teacher was pleased with his honesty.
E. Omission of the Agent (Unknown or Obvious Subjects)
When the doer of the action is unknown, indefinite (someone, people, they), or obvious, the agent (by...) is omitted:

Active: People speak English all over the world. \to Passive: English is spoken all over the world.
Active: Someone stole my pen. \to Passive: My pen was stolen.
Chapter 5: Applied Grammar — Question Tags
1. Structural Architecture & Core Mechanics
A question tag is a short question added to the end of a statement, used to confirm information or seek agreement.

                    THE FUNDAMENTAL POLARITY PRINCIPLE
                                    │
     ┌──────────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────────┐
     ▼                                                             ▼
AFFIRMATIVE STATEMENT (+)                               NEGATIVITY STATEMENT (–)
Requires a NEGATIVE TAG (–)                             Requires an AFFIRMATIVE TAG (+)
"Lamb was a bachelor, wasn't he?"                       "Yunus did not borrow money, did he?"
The Three Non-Negotiable Rules:
Pronoun Matching: The subject in a question tag must always be a personal pronoun (I, we, you, he, she, it, they). Never repeat proper or common nouns:
Incorrect: Ruskin Bond loves hills, doesn't Ruskin Bond?
Correct: Ruskin Bond loves hills, doesn't he?
Auxiliary Mirroring: The tag uses the same auxiliary/modal verb present in the main clause. If the sentence has no auxiliary (Simple Present/Simple Past), supply do, does, or did:
Statement: She plays the violin. \to Tag: doesn't she?
Statement: They visited Dehra. \to Tag: didn't they?
Contracted Negatives: Negative tags always use contracted forms (isn't, aren't, wasn't, didn't, haven't, won't, can't). Writing is not he? is grammatically incorrect.
2. Master Table of Exceptional & High-Yield Tag Formulations
Linguistic Exception	Grammatical Mechanism	High-Probability Exam Model
The "I am" Exception	Am not I does not exist in standard English; it becomes aren't I?	Statement: I am late for class, aren't I? (If negative: I am not late, am I?)
Imperative Proposals (Let's)	Suggestions starting with Let's or Let us take shall we?	Statement: Let us explore the old mansion, shall we?
Imperative Requests & Orders	Polite requests or instructions take will you? or won't you?	Statement: Shut the window, will you? / won't you?
Imperative Negative Commands	Negative commands starting with Don't take an affirmative will you?	Statement: Don't make noise in the hall, will you?
Semi-Negative Adverbs	Words like hardly, scarcely, barely, seldom, rarely make the clause negative; the tag must be affirmative (+).	Statement: Barking dogs seldom bite, do they? Statement: She hardly visits town, does she?
Negative Indefinite Pronouns	Words like few and little (without 'a') are negative, requiring an affirmative tag. (A few and a little are positive, taking a negative tag).	Statement: Few people attended, did they? Statement: A few guests arrived, didn't they?
Indefinite Personal Pronouns	Everyone, someone, nobody, no one, anybody, neither require the plural pronoun they in the tag. The auxiliary must adjust to plural.	Statement: Everybody enjoyed the story, didn't they? Statement: Nobody called, did they?
Indefinite Non-Personal Pronouns	Everything, something, nothing, anything refer to objects; the tag uses the singular pronoun it.	Statement: Nothing can go wrong, can it? Statement: Everything looks clean, doesn't it?
Introductory 'There'	Sentences introduced by There retain there as the subject in the tag.	Statement: There is no alternative route, is there?
Modal Idioms (Used to)	Takes didn't in the tag.	Statement: He used to live in Dehra, didn't he?
Chapter 6: Applied Grammar — Common Errors
1. Subject-Verb Agreement (Concord) Rules
                      SUBJECT-VERB CONCORD SPLIT
                                   │
     ┌─────────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────────┐
     ▼                                                           ▼
GOVERNED BY THE FIRST SUBJECT                             GOVERNED BY THE CLOSER SUBJECT
• as well as, along with, with                           • either...or, neither...nor
• together with, in addition to                          • not only...but also
"The teacher with his pupils HAS arrived."               "Neither the boy nor his friends WERE there."
The Cumulative Conjunction Exception: When subjects are joined by as well as, along with, together with, with, accompanied by, in addition to, the verb agrees strictly with the first subject:
Incorrect: The minister with his private secretaries have arrived.
Correct: The minister with his private secretaries has arrived.
The Proximity Rule: When subjects are joined by either...or, neither...nor, or not only...but also, the verb agrees with the closer subject:
Incorrect: Neither the teacher nor the students was present.
Correct: Neither the teacher nor the students were present.
Distributive Pronouns (Each, Every, Either, Neither): Always take a singular verb:
Incorrect: Each of the boys were given a prize.
Correct: Each of the boys was given a prize.
The "One of" Formula: Follows the pattern: \text{One of} + \text{Plural Noun} + \text{Singular Verb}
Incorrect: One of my friend have gone abroad.
Correct: One of my friends has gone abroad.
Units of Measurement, Distance, and Money: When considered as a single collective amount, they take a singular verb:
Incorrect: Ten miles are a long distance to walk.
Correct: Ten miles is a long distance to walk.
Nouns Plural in Form but Singular in Meaning: Academic subjects (Mathematics, Physics, Economics) and news take a singular verb:
Incorrect: Mathematics are a difficult subject.
Correct: Mathematics is a difficult subject.
Collective Nouns with Plural Meaning: Nouns like cattle, clergy, gentry, police, poultry take a plural verb:
Incorrect: The cattle is grazing in the meadow.
Correct: The cattle are grazing in the meadow.
2. Prepositional Usage & Idiomatic Mechanics
Latin Comparative Adjectives: Adjectives ending in -ior (senior, junior, superior, inferior, prior) along with the verb prefer take to, never than:
Incorrect: He is senior than me in service.
Correct: He is senior to me in service.
Incorrect: I prefer coffee than tea.
Correct: I prefer coffee to tea.
Transitive Verbs that Take No Preposition: Verbs like discuss, describe, order, enter, marry, resemble, sign take a direct object without prepositions:
Incorrect: We discussed about the project. \to Correct: We discussed the project.
Incorrect: He entered into the room. \to Correct: He entered the room.
Incorrect: She ordered for a cup of tea. \to Correct: She ordered a cup of tea.
Time Prepositions (Since vs. For):
Since: Used for a specific point in time (since Monday, since 1990, since morning).
For: Used for a period or duration of time (for three years, for two hours).
Incorrect: She has lived here since three years.
Correct: She has lived here for three years.
Appropriate Prepositions with Specific Words:
Good / Bad at (skills): She is good at English (not "good in").
Congratulate on: I congratulate you on your success (not "for").
Die of a disease (died of cancer); Die from a cause (died from overwork/wounds).
3. Tautology, Redundancy & Conditional Errors
Redundant Phrasing:
Incorrect: He returned back from Delhi. \to Correct: He returned from Delhi.
Incorrect: Please repeat again. \to Correct: Please repeat.
Incorrect: She is my cousin sister. \to Correct: She is my cousin.
Uncountable Nouns with No Plural Forms: Words like advice, information, furniture, luggage, scenery, hair cannot be pluralized with '-s':
Incorrect: He gave me many advices.
Correct: He gave me much advice (or pieces of advice).
Incorrect: The sceneries of Kashmir are beautiful.
Correct: The scenery of Kashmir is beautiful.
Conditional Clause Tense Errors: In a first conditional sentence, the 'if' clause uses the simple present tense, never the future tense:
Incorrect: If it will rain, the match will be cancelled.
Correct: If it rains, the match will be cancelled.
Correlative Conjunction Pairs:
Scarcely / Hardly is paired with when (never "than").
No sooner is paired with than (never "when").
Incorrect: Scarcely had he arrived than the telephone rang.
Correct: Scarcely had he arrived when the telephone rang.
Chapter 7: Applied Composition — Precis Writing
1. Conceptual Framework & Examination Rules
Definition: A precis is a clear, concise summary that captures the core meaning and logical flow of a longer text in about one-third of the original word count.
The 1/3 Length Constraint: If the original passage contains N words, the target precis length is: \text{Target Length} = \frac{N}{3} \pm 5 \text{ words}
Core Principles:
Compulsory Title: Must have a short, informative title (3 to 6 words) centered at the top that reflects the main theme.
Single Unified Paragraph: Must be written as a single, coherent paragraph regardless of how many paragraphs the original text has.
Objective Third-Person Voice: Written in the third person (the author states, the writer argues). Never use first-person pronouns (I, we, in my opinion).
Indirect Speech & Past Tense: Statements should be rendered in reported/indirect speech, usually employing past or neutral present tenses.
Own Words: Paraphrase the author's ideas; avoid copying full sentences directly from the passage.
Strict Omissions: Remove rhetorical flourishes, digressions, quotations, figures of speech, illustrations, and repetitive examples.
No Outside Additions: Do not introduce outside facts, personal opinions, or critical commentary.

3. Fully Worked Examination Model
Original Examination Passage
“The great defect of our modern civilization is that it does not know what to do with its wonderful knowledge. Science has given us powers fit for the gods, yet we use them like small children. For example, we do not know how to manage our machines. Machines were made to be man’s servants; yet he has grown so dependent upon them that they are in a fair way to become his masters. Already most men spend the greater part of their lives looking after and waiting upon machines. And the machines are very stern masters. They must be fed with coal, and given petrol to drink, and oil to wash with, and they must be kept at the right temperature. And if they do not get their meals when they expect them, they grow sulky and burst with rage, and blow up, and spread ruin and destruction all around them. So we have to wait upon them very attentively and do all that we can to keep them in a good temper.” (Original Word Count: 172 words)

Step 1: Analytical Breakdown
Central Theme: The failure of modern civilization to use scientific power wisely, leading to humanity becoming dependent on machines.
Essential Points:
Science has granted immense power, but humanity uses it without maturity.
Machines were invented to serve humans, but humans have become dependent on them.
Men spend much of their time feeding, maintaining, and caring for machines.
Neglecting machines causes breakdowns and widespread destruction, making machines the masters of men.
Items to Omit: Figurative descriptions ("powers fit for the gods," "given petrol to drink," "oil to wash with," "grow sulky and burst with rage").
Length Target: \frac{172}{3} \approx 57 \text{ words} (acceptable range: 52 to 62 words).
Step 2: Final Model Precis
Man’s Subservience to Modern Machines

Modern civilization has failed to use its scientific knowledge wisely. Although machines were invented to serve humanity, humans have become entirely dependent on them. People now spend much of their lives feeding, maintaining, and tending to machines. Consequently, machines have become demanding masters, and any failure to maintain them results in widespread industrial ruin.
